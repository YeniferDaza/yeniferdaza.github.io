A custom fit-out service line, built from nothing and then run across 38 client projects

A furniture manufacturer kept being asked for work it had no way of delivering: interiors measured, made and installed in someone’s own home or premises. I built the service line that made it a repeatable offer, then coordinated each project through it — site measurement, design, suppliers, installers and schedule — to installed handover.

Context
Driftwood — ready-to-assemble furniture manufacturer, Colombia
My role
Developed the service line and coordinated delivery, project by project
Timeframe

01

Project overview

A catalogue product is the same for everybody. A fit-out is not: it is measured in one specific room, in a building that is never square, for a client who will be living in it while the work happens. The company was good at the first thing and kept being asked for the second.

Saying yes once is easy. Saying yes thirty-eight times, to thirty-eight different rooms, without the workshop losing its rhythm or a client losing a promised date, is a service line — a defined sequence, a known set of documents, and someone accountable for the handoffs. That is what this project was.

Why this project matters

It is the clearest example in this portfolio of building the capability and then running it: the same person defined how the work would flow and then carried projects through it, repeatedly, to an installed result.

02

Need & challenge

Custom work arrived project by project, with nothing in common between one and the next. That produced four problems at once:

  • No repeatable route. Each project was solved from the beginning — how it was quoted, drawn, made, scheduled and installed.
  • Two production logics in one workshop. Catalogue products run in batches; a fit-out runs to one date in one room. They compete for the same machines and the same people.
  • Dependencies outside the building. Suppliers, installers and site trades all had to land in the right order, on a site the company did not control.
  • A client living with the work. The end of a fit-out is not a despatch note. It is a handover, in person, in a finished room.
03

My role

This was a team delivery, and the boundary matters here more than on any other page in this portfolio: the drawings, renders and 3D models for these projects were overwhelmingly produced by the company’s design team, not by me. My work was the service and its coordination.

  • Owned: the service line — defining how a custom project is taken from first client contact to installed handover, and what exists at each step.
  • Owned: site visits and measurement, the requirements that came out of them, the budget, and the project schedule.
  • Coordinated: suppliers, the workshop, installers and site trades against the install date, project by project, and resolved the issues that surfaced on site.
  • Coordinated: the material ordering behind individual projects, which is evidenced in the ordering record under my own identity.
  • Did not own: the design drawings, renders and 3D models, the fabrication itself, or the installation labour.

A coordinator leaves very few files behind, and that is exactly what the record shows here: the drawings on these projects are other people’s. What is mine is that thirty-eight of them reached a client’s room and were handed over.

04

How I worked

Every project ran the same five steps, in the same order, whatever its size.

  • Measure before promising. Site visit first, with dimensions and finishes recorded on a drawing the workshop and the installers would both work from. Nothing is quoted from a photograph.
  • Turn the room into requirements. What the client wants, what the building allows and what the workshop can make, reconciled once, in writing, before design begins.
  • Schedule backwards from the install date. The install date is the fixed point; supply, fabrication and trades are placed behind it, not in front of it.
  • Hold the suppliers and the trades together. A fit-out fails at the joints between people, so the coordination effort goes there rather than into the drawing.
  • Close it in the room. Handover happens on site, with the client, in the finished space — and the finished space is photographed.

07

Supporting evidence

  • Material ordering for individual projects. Project-specific timber ordering appears in the same ordering record described in the material supply project — the same hands, on both sides of the workshop door.
  • The photographic record of installed work. Held, and shown unnamed. Several projects are photographed on completion, empty of the client’s belongings, which is what makes anonymisation clean.
  • Brand and presentation material. The service line needed to be presentable to a client before it existed; the company’s own identity and catalogue material carried that.
08

What I would do differently

  • Close each project with a one-page record. Promised date against actual, budget against final, and what went wrong. Thirty-eight projects went past and the only surviving evidence of how they went is photographs of the good outcome.
  • Get the client to sign the handover. A signed acceptance takes two minutes and settles every later conversation about scope.
  • Protect the workshop from the install date. Custom projects and catalogue batches competed for the same capacity, and the fit-out always won because it had a client waiting in a room. A reserved capacity block would have been fairer to both.
