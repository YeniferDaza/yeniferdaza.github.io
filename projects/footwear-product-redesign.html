A footwear manufacturer’s product redesigned for direct injection

Brought in from outside to fix a product that would not run consistently — and the process behind it documented, trained and left running. I redesigned the product and its patterns for direct-injection construction, wrote the technical and process specifications, trained the production teams on them, carried pilot lots into series production, and developed a new women’s safety-footwear line along the way.

Context
Calzado 3025 — safety and industrial footwear manufacturer, Colombia
My role
Independent consultant, contracted directly by the manufacturer

01

Project overview

Direct injection bonds the sole to the upper by injecting polyurethane straight onto it in a mould. It is fast and it is unforgiving: the upper has to be cut, assembled and lasted within tolerances the mould will accept, every pair, or the defect appears at the last operation, when all the material has already been spent.

I was contracted from outside the company to make that work. The brief looked like a product problem and was really a process one, so the deliverable ended up being neither a drawing nor a report but a way of working that the floor could keep running after I left.

Why this project matters

It is the full arc in one engagement — diagnose, redesign, document, train, run, and put a control in place — done as an outsider with no authority over anyone, which is the condition most coordination work actually happens in.

02

Need & challenge

Product was reaching the end of the line and failing there. The causes were spread across three places that rarely talk to each other:

  • The patterns. Pieces cut to a shape that worked for a stitched sole did not hold their geometry once the last and the mould were involved.
  • The specifications. What existed described the product, not how to make it — no calibres, no sequence, no acceptance criterion, nothing dated.
  • The practice. Each operator had a reasonable method, and no two were the same. Without a written standard there was nothing to train against and nothing to inspect against.
  • And an outsider’s constraint. I could not instruct anyone. Anything that was going to last had to be adopted, not imposed.
03

My role

I was contracted directly, working hands-on inside the manufacturer’s own operation. The company’s quality system, its certification and its commercial results are the manufacturer’s; what follows is what I did.

  • Owned: the redesign of the product and its patterns for direct-injection construction, carried through successive versions until the set was stable.
  • Owned: the technical and process specifications — materials against testing standards, operation sequence, calibres and acceptance criteria — issued with a version number and an effective date.
  • Owned: the development of a new women’s safety-footwear line, from requirements and materials through to pilot production.
  • Coordinated: pilot lots with the production teams, correcting the model against what the floor actually produced rather than against the drawing.
  • Delivered: training for the production teams on the new specifications, on the floor and against the real operation.
  • Developed: the product design-process content the company worked to — context, research, concept, detail design, development — with alignment gates between stages, working with the Quality Manager who documented it from the quality-management side.
  • Helped integrate: the design process into the manufacturer’s quality management system, which the Quality Manager was documenting from the quality side.
  • Did not own: manufacturing, purchasing, the workforce, or any company-level result.

An outsider gets one kind of authority: being right in writing, early enough to be useful. Everything I could make stick here was something that had been written down, dated, and tested on a real lot before anyone was asked to believe it.

04

How I worked

Four moves, in order, and the order is the method.

  • Diagnose at the operation, not at the desk. The failure showed at injection; the cause was upstream, in cutting and in skiving calibre. Watching the operation is what separated the two.
  • Redesign, then version. The pattern set was corrected and reissued as versions rather than replaced quietly — so anyone could tell which set a lot had been cut from.
  • Write the specification as an instruction. Each process sheet says what to do, to what calibre, in what order, and from what date it applies. A sheet without an effective date is a suggestion.
  • Train on the floor, then pilot. Teams were trained on the new sheets, a pilot lot was run, the model was corrected against it, and only then did it go to series.
  • Leave a control behind. Non-conformance was checked stage by stage rather than only at the end, so a defect is caught where it is made and by the person who can act on it.

The part I would point a hiring manager at is the least dramatic one: the specifications have dates on them. A dated, versioned instruction is the difference between an improvement that happened once and a process that keeps happening.

07

Supporting evidence

  • The consumption and cost sheet. Consumption per pair, waste, factor, components and unit cost for a named model — the same discipline applied at cost level, and the reason a specification change could be priced before it was approved.
  • Quality-management support. I helped integrate the design process into the manufacturer’s quality management system. Their controlled forms are theirs; they are cited here as context and are not reproduced.
  • Training on the floor. Delivered against the process sheets above. No training record was kept at the time, which is noted below rather than claimed.
08

What I would do differently

  • Fix the measurement before fixing the product. The single thing I would change is baselining the defect rate, with a stated sample and period, before any redesign — so the effect of the work could be shown rather than described.
  • Keep the training record. Sessions were delivered and nothing was signed. An attendance sheet against each version of each process sheet would have cost minutes and would still be evidence today.
  • Agree the handover point in writing. As an external consultant I left the documents in force and the control running, with no formal moment where the manufacturer took ownership of maintaining them.
