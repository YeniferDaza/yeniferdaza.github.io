Multiple product lines coordinated from design intent to fabrication

I researched what a compact-housing market needed, set the product direction that came out of it, and developed the catalogue to one standard — 3D models, renders, packaging and fabrication drawings, and the assembly manual. Then I trained a junior designer and handed over its highest-volume element. The last two years of it were coordinated remotely, from Canada.

01

Project overview

Driftwood sold ready-to-assemble furniture online. That business model has one hard consequence: every product has to arrive at a customer’s door as a set of cut parts, a box that protects them, and a manual someone can follow alone on a living-room floor. Nothing can be finished on site, and nothing can be explained in person.

So each product had to be carried the whole way — from a base model through dimensioned drawings, an assembly sequence, renders for the marketplace listing, packaging drawings and a printed manual — and it had to be done the same way every time, across a catalogue of multiple product lines. This case study is about deciding what to develop, developing it to one standard, and handing it over.

Why this project matters

Four years of continuous product work, the last two remote from Canada, ending in a handover that held.

02

Need & challenge

A growing catalogue built by more than one designer drifts. Two products in the same line end up documented differently, the workshop asks a question a drawing should have answered, and a customer receives a manual that assumes knowledge they do not have.

The problem had four parts at once:

  • One product, many documents. A single product code needed a base model, a dimensioned model, an assembly model, renders, a packaging drawing, a fabrication sheet and a manual — each correct against the others.
  • More than one hand. Several designers worked inside the same collections, so the standard had to live in the files rather than in a conversation.
  • Assembly by a stranger. The manual is the only support a ready-to-assemble customer gets. It is a design deliverable, not an afterthought.
  • Continuity through a relocation. Partway through, I moved to Canada. The chain had to keep running across a time difference, without me in the workshop.
03

My role

The catalogue was built by a team. The company used an author marker in its file-naming convention, so the work below is traceable to me.

  • Owned: the product direction behind the 2024 range — market and housing research from published statistics, a customer profile, five defined market needs and the product classification that came out of them. Produced remotely, from Canada.
  • Owned: the product development set — 3D base, dimensioned and assembly models, renders and room scenes, packaging and fabrication drawings, manual — across the two collections that carry my marker.
  • Coordinated: the sequence between design, costing, the workshop and the sales channels, so a product reached a listing with its documentation already complete.
  • Contributed to: collections led by other designers, where my marker appears on part of the set rather than all of it.
  • Did not own: manufacturing, and the company’s commercial results.

The useful thing I did was not drawing furniture. It was deciding what “finished” means for a product, writing it down as a set of documents, and then making that set the normal amount of work.

04

How I worked

  • Research before range. How much floor space the customer actually had decided what was worth developing, before anything was drawn.
  • One code per product, carried through every document. The same product code appears on the model, the render, the manual, the budget and the purchase order — so any document can be traced to any other without asking anyone.
  • A fixed set per product. Base model, dimensioned model, assembly model, renders, packaging drawing, fabrication sheet, manual — design, visualization and manufacturing information produced as one set.
  • The author marker in the filename. Who produced each file is visible in the file itself, which makes a shared catalogue auditable.
  • Documentation before handover, not after. When the highest-volume part of the catalogue was handed to a junior designer I had trained, what transferred was a documented standard rather than a set of habits.
  • Remote by written artifact. From Canada, coordination ran on the documents rather than on presence — which only works if the documents were already doing the work.

06

Result

The company’s commercial results were a team outcome. What is mine is the product direction, the development standard, and the fact that both kept running without me.

One standard Applied across every product code in the two collections that carry my marker
Handed over The highest-volume element transferred to a designer I trained
Four years Continuous coordination, the last two from Canada

A standard that only works while its author is present is a habit. This one was picked up by someone else, applied to the busiest part of the catalogue, and continued while I coordinated remotely from Canada.

07

Supporting evidence

Three strands sit inside this project rather than beside it.

  • The collections themselves. The product development behind two collections — concepts, models, renders and room scenes — is the same work seen close up. It is this project’s content, not a second project.
  • Product criteria and training. I defined four product-development criteria — functionality, aesthetics, affordability and differentiation — which also became part of the framework used to train the junior designer.
  • Training and handover. A junior designer trained through hands-on guidance and review, then given the highest-volume part of the catalogue.
  • The fabrication procedures. The version-controlled standard procedures for product fabrication, box fabrication and packing are the workshop-facing end of this same documentation chain.
08

What I would do differently

  • Write the standard down as a document, not only into the files. The convention was real and consistently applied, but it lived in practice. A one-page specification would have made it teachable in an afternoon instead of over months.
  • Version the manuals as strictly as the drawings. Fabrication documents carried versions; customer-facing manuals were treated as finished once published, which is optimistic.
  • Record the handover. The training worked and left no artifact. A short checklist of what was transferred would be worth more now than any drawing on this page.
