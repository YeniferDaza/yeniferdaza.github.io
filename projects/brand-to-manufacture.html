Footwear and accessory brands taken from product idea to manufacture

Emerging brands arrive with a product they can describe and no route to making it: no specification, no costed consumption, no supplier who will take the order. I was contracted directly by brand owners to close that gap — requirements, sourcing, costing, prototypes, delivery scheduling and pilot runs — across several brands over five years.

01

Project overview

A small brand with a good idea usually fails at the same place. Not at design, and not at selling — at the join between them, where an idea has to become a specification a factory will quote, a consumption a price can be built from, and an order a supplier will actually schedule.

For five years I was the person brands hired to cross that join. The work was the same shape each time and the products were never the same: boots, accessories, corporate lines. What travelled between them was the method, not the product.

Why this project matters

It is breadth rather than depth — several independent clients, each taken the full distance from idea to manufactured product, with no organisation behind me and no authority except the work.

02

Need & challenge

Each client arrived with a different product and the same four gaps:

  • No specification. A reference image and a description are not something a factory can quote, cut or assemble.
  • No costed consumption. Without material consumption per unit, a price is a guess — and the first production run is where the guess gets expensive.
  • No supplier route. Small volumes are unattractive to manufacturers. Getting a first run scheduled is a relationship problem as much as a technical one.
  • No sequence. Prototype, correction, pilot run, production — brands frequently tried to skip from the first to the last.
03

My role

I was contracted directly by the brand owners, as an individual. The brands, their designs and their commercial results are theirs; what follows is the part I was hired to do and did.

  • Owned: turning the client’s intent into requirements and a technical specification a manufacturer could work from.
  • Owned: the costed consumption sheet — material per unit, waste, factor, components and unit cost — which is what let the client price the product before committing to a run.
  • Owned: prototyping and the corrections between prototype and pilot.
  • Coordinated: sourcing, suppliers, production orders and delivery scheduling, following each run through to despatch.
  • Did not own: the brands themselves, their design identity, their pricing decisions, their marketing, or the manufacturing.

Clients did not hire me to design their product. They hired me because their product could not be made yet, and the distance between those two things is where all of this work lives.

04

How I worked

  • Specification before sourcing. No supplier is approached until the product is described in materials, components and construction. It changes the conversation from a favour to a quotation.
  • Consumption before price. Material per unit, waste and factor, built as a sheet the client keeps. A price that is not built on consumption is not a price.
  • Prototype, correct, then pilot. The pilot run exists to prove the specification survives the factory floor, not to fill an order.
  • Schedule against the client’s date. Production orders issued in sequence, with delivery followed through rather than assumed.
  • Leave the documents with the client. The specification and the consumption sheet stay with the brand, so the second run does not need me.

07

Supporting evidence

  • Technical product design carried into production. The same five-year period includes model and colourway development taken through to annotated production patterns with per-piece cut quantities — the technical end of this same practice.
  • Finished, branded product. At least one client’s product exists as a manufactured, branded article. It is not shown here without the client’s permission.
  • Costing discipline. The consumption sheet format used here is the same one I was still building product cost models with six years later, in furniture.
08

What I would do differently

  • Agree what the engagement ends with, in writing, at the start. Several of these relationships drifted into ongoing availability rather than closing cleanly on a delivered result.
  • Keep a portfolio copy of every deliverable. Work done for a client leaves with the client. Five years of specifications and cost sheets exist somewhere, and almost none of it is mine to show.
  • Ask for permission to reference the work at the point of signing — not years later, which is why this page is written without names.
